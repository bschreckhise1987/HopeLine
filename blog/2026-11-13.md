# From Draft to Sprint: Planning Week in Review

**November 13, 2026**

**Author: Brandy Schreckhise**

---

Not every week in software development is a coding week — and this was one of them.

The past few days were about the professional side of the craft: taking feedback on a written document and turning it into a final draft, sketching the app as a prototype wireframe, and setting up the agile workflow that will carry HopeLine through the rest of the capstone. It's the kind of work that doesn't show up in a screenshot of a beautiful UI, but it's the work that makes everything after it possible.

Here's the week in review.

---

## 📄 The Final Draft

The week started with feedback. My rough draft of the Software Development Document came back with instructor comments, and my job was to turn that feedback into a final draft.

Receiving feedback on a document you've poured yourself into is one of the most underrated skills in this field. The temptation is to defend what you wrote. The discipline is to read every comment as data — where was I unclear? What did a reader need that I didn't give them? Where did I assume knowledge the reader didn't have?

I worked through the comments one by one, made the corrections, and submitted the final draft. Done is better than perfect, but feedback-informed done is better still.

---

## 🖼️ The Prototype Wireframe

With the document finalized, I built a prototype wireframe — a mockup of HopeLine's screens.

A wireframe is deliberately not a polished design. It's the skeleton: where each screen lives, what sits on it, and how a user moves from one place to the next. Home, Daily Check-In, Journal, Prayer, Meetings, Scripture, Settings — the whole flow of the app, sketched out so the structure can be evaluated before any of it gets built (or rebuilt).

The value of a wireframe is that changes cost nothing. If a screen's flow feels wrong on paper, you redraw a box. If it feels wrong in code, you rewrite a view. Catching structural problems at the mockup stage is the cheapest quality assurance there is.

---

## 🗂️ Setting Up the Agile Workflow

The biggest project of the week: setting up the Jira board and populating the backlog.

This is where the whole capstone got organized. Starting from the requirements list in the Software Development Document, I wrote out every task the project needs — **35 backlog items across 10 epics**, covering authentication, check-ins, journaling, prayer, meetings, Scripture, settings, crisis support, progress tracking, and release readiness.

Each item got:

- **A detailed description** — enough context that a reviewer understands the task without asking questions
- **Completion criteria** — an objective, measurable test that answers "how do we know this task is complete?"
- **Story points** — on this project's scale, one point equals one hour of expected work
- **A status** — reflecting what's actually built so far

That last part was the most satisfying. Marking statuses against the real app showed that **24 of the 35 items are already done** — the authentication system, the daily check-in, the journal, the prayer wall, the nationwide meeting finder, the entire Scripture feature, and the new Settings screen. Two more are started, nine are to come.

The backlog is honest: it reflects where HopeLine actually is, not where I wish it was.

---

## 🏃 Starting the Week 4 Sprint

With the backlog in place, I planned and started the Week 4 sprint.

Four items, 44 story points, in a realistic order:

1. **Saved Scriptures list in Settings** (12 pts) — replacing the placeholder with the user's real saved verses
2. **My Data screen with live counts** (12 pts) — journal entries, check-ins, saved Scriptures, prayer activity
3. **Meeting detail screen** (16 pts) — full information for any meeting in the list
4. **Privacy Policy and Terms screens** (4 pts) — the documentation screens linked from Settings

The first two finish work that's already started. The third is the biggest new build. The fourth fills the sprint's remaining room. Every item has dependencies identified, and the order reflects which tasks unblock which.

The sprint starts tomorrow, in Xcode.

---

## 🔮 Why This Week Mattered

Three months ago, this project was an idea. Now it has a finished requirements document, a wireframe of its entire user flow, a backlog where every task is defined, estimated, and measurable, and a sprint plan that says exactly what the next week of development will produce.

Planning weeks feel slow when you're in them. But walking into a coding week knowing precisely what you're building, in what order, and how you'll know when each piece is done — that's not slowness. That's speed you deposit in advance.

Next post should be a coding week again: four sprint items, one week, one check-in at the end.
